# Xiangqi School v0.3 — Online play design

> This file is the master copy of this design document. Update it here, in the same commit as the change it describes.

Oct 11, 2026 · Jet Zhu · **Draft for discussion**

v0.3 lets signed-in players play each other in real time. Three goals shape the design:

1. **Fair matches.** Opponents of about the same level, found quickly even when few people are online.
2. **Fair play.** Make cheating hard and unrewarding, catch it from the evidence, and never drive away an honest player who is falsely accused.
3. **Free to run.** It all fits Supabase's free plan and GitHub Pages, like the rest of the site.

It builds on the requirements study (R11 matchmaking, R24 anti-cheat, R41 fair timing, R45 game controls, R46 Xiangqi rulings, R63 safety) and on research done on 2026-10-11 into chess.com's help center, lichess's published rules and source code, Tiantian Xiangqi's fair-play notices, Ken Regan's statistical cheat-detection work, and Supabase's free-plan limits. Sources are at the end.

## Speeds and time controls

Xiangqi players know these as 闪电 / 超快棋 / 快棋. A time control is written **base + increment**: "5+3" means 5 minutes each to start, and **3 seconds added to your clock after every move you make** (Fischer timing). A game's speed is its estimated length, base + 40 × increment, the formula both chess.com and lichess use:

| Speed | Estimated length | Quick-pair tiles (main tile in bold) | First-move timer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lightning 闪电 | under 3 min | 1+2, **2+1** | 20 s |
| Blitz 超快棋 | 3 to under 10 min | 3+2, **5+3** | 30 s |
| Rapid 快棋 | 10 min and over | **10+5**, 15+10 | 45 s |

**Every quick-pair tile has an increment** (settled 2026-10-11), so the speeds differ in the time added per move as well as the starting time, and the increment grows with the speed:

- A player who moves quickly never runs completely dry, so a won game isn't lost in a time scramble: games are decided by the moves.
- It absorbs some network delay, which helps players on mobile connections.
- Lightning stays too fast to relay engine moves from another device, even with +1 or +2 s.
- The main tile of each speed is pre-selected on the Play page.

- Each speed has its own rating, because people play differently at each speed.
- A custom challenge allows any base from 1 to 60 min and any increment from 0 to 30 s, with an increment pre-filled; its speed follows from the formula.
- **Why only three speeds:** a small site has few players online at once, and every extra pool splits them further. Chess.com's ±200 window and lichess's waves assume thousands of players; here six tiles over three ratings is already plenty.
- **Lightning resists relaying.** Moving pieces between a phone running an engine and the board (the "RICE" pattern in the brief) costs several seconds a move, which Lightning doesn't leave. Fair-play checks still apply, with different expectations (see below).
- **Later:** per-move time (步时 / byo-yomi), correspondence games and tournaments. Correspondence is where outside help is easiest and slowest to detect, so it waits until the fair-play system has proved itself.

## Finding an opponent

### Quick pairing

The player picks a tile. The server pairs players of the same tile, nearest rating first, and among equal matches whoever waited longest.

- The rating window starts at **±50** and widens by 25 every 5 s up to **±200** (chess.com's cap for most players). A provisional player (rating uncertainty still high) starts at ±150.
- **When nobody fits** (settled 2026-10-11), the search shows the nearest players also seeking, e.g. "Closest: lin_88, 1420 (+260), waiting 40 s in 5+3", each with **Play anyway**. One click sends that player "A player rated 1160 (−260) is ready to play you" with Accept / Decline for 15 s; they're paired only when both accept. **The search doesn't pause meanwhile:** while an invitation is pending, both players stay in the normal search, and if a normal match turns up first for either of them, it happens and the invitation is withdrawn (an Accept then shows "No longer available, still searching…"). Pairing and accepting go through the same database step, which takes both players out of the queue in one transaction, so nobody ends up in two games. Only the rating range is widened this way: blocks, fair-play filters and quiet separate matching still apply. Glicko keeps a wide pairing fair: the stronger player gains little for a win and loses a lot for a loss.
- After 30 s the page also offers to widen to **anyone**, to switch tile ("3 players are waiting in 5+3"), or to **play a bot while waiting**; the search keeps going and the bot game becomes casual if a match is found.
- Colours alternate: the player who had Red more often in recent games gets Black.
- The page shows how many players are seeking in each tile, so a quiet site doesn't feel broken.

### Challenge a friend

A link (and a short code to read out) that starts a game with chosen time, colour and rated or casual. It's the most reliable way to get a game on a small site, so it comes first in the milestones.

### Opponent filters

Set in Settings → Play, and applied both ways (you're only matched with someone whose filters also accept you), as on chess.com and lichess. Each filter shows how many players currently seeking would pass it, since narrow filters mean longer waits.

| Filter | Options | Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rating range | −400 … +400 around your rating, or any | ±200 |
| Established players only | Hide provisional opponents (fewer than ~10 rated games at this speed) | Off |
| Account age | Any, 7 days, 30 days | Any |
| Completion rate (棋品) | Any, 90 %, 95 % | Any |
| Fair-play checked | Any, or at least 10 checked games with no finding | Any |
| Blocked players | Never matched, can't challenge you | — |

## Ratings

- Glicko, as the bot and puzzle ratings already use, one rating per speed.
- **Starting rating:** the player's bot rating if they have 10 or more rated bot games, otherwise the skill answer from sign-up (800 if skipped); high uncertainty, shown with a "?" until it settles (lichess clears it below an uncertainty of 110).
- Only rated games of at least 4 plies count, the same rule as bot games.
- A leaderboard per speed needs 30 rated games, a settled rating, play in the last 30 days, and at least 10 fair-play checked games.
- Rating floor: 100.

## The game

### Controls by moment

| Moment | Controls | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before each side's first move | Abort | First-move timer per speed; if it runs out the game is aborted, unrated. |
| Playing | Resign (confirm), Offer draw, preset chat; Takeback request in casual games only | — |
| Draw offer | Accept / Decline next to the clock, no pop-up | Rated: from 10 moves each (settled 2026-10-11), which stops quick agreed draws used to swap rating points; casual: any time. Moving declines it. The offerer waits 10 moves before offering again (lichess). |
| Takeback request | Accept / Decline | Casual only; each refusal lengthens the wait before the next request (10 s, 40 s, 90 s…). |
| Opponent disconnected | Countdown banner, then Claim win / Claim draw / Keep waiting | See Disconnects. |
| Game over | Result and reason in plain words, rating change, Rematch, New game, Game Review, Report | Analysis and Game Review unlock only now. |

- **Xiangqi rulings** come from `xiangqi-core`: checkmate, stalemate (a loss), perpetual check and chase, repetition and the natural move limit, stated in plain words ("Red loses: perpetual check").
- **No help during rated or casual human games:** no hints, eval bar, move feedback or threat arrows. While a game is in progress in this browser, the site's own analysis board shows "Analysis is off while you have a game in progress", and the game page notices if the site's engine starts in another tab.
- **Premoves** for Lightning and Blitz (one queued move); **confirm move** stays available for Rapid.

### Disconnects and reconnecting

- **Clocks never stop**, as the brief asks. Both browsers keep both clocks, and every move is also stamped on arrival in the server's log. A page that reloads, or a player who comes back on another device, gets the moves and clock readings from the opponent's browser over a new link (or from the server's log) and carries on.
- **Reconnect window** = (base + 40 × increment) ÷ 10, at least 30 s and at most 3 min (chess.com's and lichess's formula). It's halved for a player down 4 or more points of material (lichess), and cut to 10 s if they closed the tab.
- **Who notices a disconnect** (settled 2026-10-11): the players, not the server. The two browsers exchange heartbeats every second or two over their direct link (see Architecture), which costs nothing because it doesn't touch the server; moves are signs of life too. (A game relayed through the server uses Presence instead, as Broadcast heartbeats would use up the free plan's messages.) The server is asked only when a player reports the opponent gone: the reporter is evidently connected, so the absent player's disconnect timer starts, and their return cancels it. If both report each other, both are connected and only the link broke: the server has the browsers set up a new link, or fall back to the relay.
- **Longer games** (window over 1 min 30 s, e.g. 15+10): after **1 min** the connected player may keep waiting or **claim**; a claim gives the absent player a **last 30 s** to come back, then the game is theirs. Without a claim the window runs to its end (at most 3 min) and the claim is then instant.
- If it's the connected player's turn, their own clock runs: they move, and then the absent player's clock and disconnect timer run. The clock always decides first: an absent player whose time runs out loses on time.
- When the window ends, the waiting player may **claim the win** (a draw if they don't have enough material to mate), **claim a draw**, or keep waiting. If **both** players are gone for more than the window, the game is a draw with no rating change.
- **Lag:** players are charged only for thinking time. The network delay, measured from the heartbeats' round trip, is credited up to a small allowance per move with a bank that refills (chess.com: 200 / 300 / 500 ms per move by speed). A dishonest browser gains at most that allowance.
- A connection indicator next to each name shows the measured round trip; a clock that jumps after a lag correction shows a brief "adjusted for lag" note.

### Sportsmanship (棋品)

Bad behaviour hurts honest players almost as much as cheating, and unlike cheating it can be measured exactly. Each player's last 30 games are labelled:

| Behaviour | How it's recognised |
| --- | --- |
| Abandoning | Left and didn't return within the reconnect window. |
| Stalling | In a lost game, let the clock run down after idling longer than the reconnect window (lichess's "sitting"). |
| Frequent aborts | Aborted more than a few of the last 10 games after the opponent was seen. |
| Long first moves | Spent over half the base time on one of the first 10 moves (chess.com). |

- **Completion rate** = games finished normally (played to a result) ÷ games started, last 90 days. It's public, filterable, and only ever moves because of the player's own actions, so it can't be manipulated by opponents.
- **Penalties are private and gentle**, as on both big sites: a warning to acknowledge, then a pairing wait (10 min, multiplied for repeats within 3 days, at most a day), and matching with other players who do the same.

## Fair play

### The aim

Stop cheating paying off, without making honest players feel watched or accused. The research gives four principles that every big site follows:

1. **One game proves nothing.** Decisions come from several games compared with honest players of the same rating, never from a fixed percentage of "engine moves" (Regan: a fair 2100 player matches the engine's first choice about half the time).
2. **Reports start a review, they never decide it.** Chess.com dismisses all reports when no cheating is found, however many there are.
3. **Only confirmed findings are public.** No big site shows report counts or suspicion scores; they'd be a weapon for sore losers.
4. **A human confirms before anything public happens.**

### What's recorded

Recorded for every online game, cheaply, and said plainly in the fair-play policy:

| Data | Per | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Server receipt time and reported think time | Move | Clocks, lag allowance, and the timing checks below |
| Focus bit: did the page lose focus between the opponent's move and this one? | Move | A player looking at another window every move (lichess's "blurs") |
| Engine analysis: loss against the best move, match with the engine's first choice, how many good moves there were (position difficulty) | Move, after the game | Accuracy against players of the same rating |
| Sportsmanship labels | Game | Completion rate and penalties |

Not recorded: mouse movement, clipboard, other programs, camera. They're intrusive and easily fooled.

### Who analyses the games, for free

There's no server that can run an engine for free, so the analysis runs where engines already run: in browsers.

- **Both players' browsers** analyse the finished game at a fixed depth with one thread, which gives the same numbers on every machine. Players get their Game Review from it anyway.
- **The two results are compared.** A cheater who fakes the analysis of their own game is contradicted by the opponent's browser. If only one browser sends results, the game waits for a second opinion from the moderator's review page (below).
- Games of **players rated 1800 and above, and anyone on a leaderboard, are always analysed**, as the brief asks. If neither player's browser did it, they go to the review queue.

### What counts as suspicious

Each signal compares a player with honest players of the same rating and speed; the site builds those baselines from its own games. The exact thresholds stay private, as on every big site.

| Signal | Honest players | Engine help |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy and first-choice match, middle-game moves only (about plies 16–120, skipping forced and book moves) | Spread around their rating's average | Far above it, game after game |
| Time against difficulty | Quick on obvious moves, long on hard ones | About the same time for every move; slow on obvious recaptures (the RICE signature) |
| Spread of move times | Varied | Unusually even (lichess flags a spread below 25 % of the average) |
| Fast moves | Some moves under half a second | Almost none |
| Focus lost | Now and then | On nearly every move |
| Results | Rating rises gradually | Sudden jump; wins against much stronger players |

- A game is scored from these together, and a player from their last 10–20 games: a "likely" verdict needs several games far out of line (Regan's threshold for statistics alone is about 5 standard deviations; lower values only add weight to other evidence).
- Games under 36 plies aren't scored, and a game the suspect lost counts for less (lichess).
- **Partial cheating** (a few engine moves in the critical moments) is the hardest case; scoring the middle game separately and weighting difficult positions helps.
- **Lightning** is scored with looser accuracy expectations and needs more games, because fast games are noisier.

### Reports

- **Who can report:** only a player of that game, within 7 days, once per game, at most 5 reports a day.
- **Reasons:** engine or outside help, stalling, abusive chat (presets can still be used to taunt), offensive username, other (with a short note).
- **Weight:** each reporter has a track record (reports confirmed ÷ reports made). Reports from accurate reporters move a case up the queue; reports from someone who reports every loss count for little; reporters with a confirmed violation of their own count for nothing. Persistent false reporting can lose the right to report.
- **A report starts work at once:** the suspect's recent unanalysed games go to the review queue.
- **The reporter sees:** "Thank you. Every report is reviewed; you'll be told if action is taken." If it is, they get a notice (no details) and any rating they lost to that player back.

### On the profile: what other players can see

The brief asks whether the number of times a player was reported should be on their profile so others can filter it out. The research argues against the raw count: one angry opponent, or a group of them, can make it climb without any cheating, and that drives away exactly the honest players we want to keep. Proposal: show evidence that **only the player's own actions** can change, and let filters use it.

| Shown on the profile | Can be filtered | Moved by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Completion rate 棋品** (e.g. 98 %) | Yes | The player's own aborts, abandonments and stalling |
| **Fair-play checked games** (e.g. "52 checked, none flagged") | Yes | Analysis of their games |
| **Fair-play violation**, with the date (only if confirmed by a moderator) | Yes ("hide") | A moderator's decision |
| Account age, rated games per speed, provisional "?" | Yes | Time and play |
| Raw report count, suspicion score | **No: never shown** | — |

Settled on 2026-10-11: the block is called **Fair-play record / 公平记录**, with **棋品** as the label of the completion rate. Raw reports stay private; if a figure is ever wanted, the safe form is upheld reports (confirmed by a moderator), which honest players can't collect.

### Sanctions and appeals

| Level | When | What happens | Public? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quiet | Score "likely", not yet reviewed | Matched only with other flagged players for rated games (lichess's separate pools); games queued for review | No |
| First confirmed violation | Moderator confirms | Rated online play suspended 30 days; ratings reset to provisional; **victims refunded** (rating lost to them in the last 3 months, up to 100 games, as chess.com); reporters told; public mark for 1 year | Yes |
| Second confirmed violation | Moderator confirms | Online play suspended at once; the player has **30 days to appeal**; the account is closed for online play only if the appeal is rejected or the 30 days pass. The mark stays. | Yes |

- **Appeal** at every level, by email within 30 days, reviewed again with fresh eyes; at the second level no closure is final before the appeal is decided. Tiantian Xiangqi offers a supervised test match at the player's level; a version of that (a watched game against a trusted player) is an option for disputed cases.
- Every moderator action goes in an append-only log with the reason and the evidence.

### Moderation

At first the site owner is the only moderator. A **review page** (moderators only) shows, for each case: the games with the engine graph, time per move against difficulty, focus bits, the player's numbers against the same-rating baseline, and the reports with their reporters' track records. It runs the engine in the moderator's browser to fill gaps. A small player base means few cases; the queue is ordered so the strongest evidence and the highest-rated players come first.

## Safety

- **Guests** can't play online, as planned in v0.2.
- **Before the first online game** (settled 2026-10-11): a verified email, and either
  - a win against a bot rated at or above the player's level (their rating, or the skill answer from sign-up; the strongest bot for anyone above it) without hints or takebacks, or
  - 3 completed bot games of at least 10 moves each, any result.

  Bot games run in the browser, so this doesn't prove honesty; it makes every new account cost time, which slows down throwaway accounts, and it gives a first look at the player's level. The Play page shows the progress ("2 of 3 games played").
- **Chat:** preset bilingual phrases only ("Good luck", "Good game", "Thanks", "Oops", "Well played"…), switchable off. No free-text chat in v0.3, so no profanity filter or chat moderation is needed yet.
- **Block** a player: no matching, no challenges.
- **Public profile** (new in v0.3): username, avatar, country flag, ratings per speed, completion rate, fair-play checked games and any confirmed violation. Players under 16 keep a minimal public profile (username and ratings only).
- A **fair-play policy** and a **sportsmanship policy** page, linked from the sign-up and Play pages, say what's forbidden, what's recorded and how decisions and appeals work.

## Architecture

The rest of the site doesn't change: a static site on GitHub Pages, Supabase for accounts and data.

**Settled on 2026-10-12: the two browsers play the game directly with each other; the server keeps a record, judges disputes and rates the result.** The server is needed to find an opponent and start a game. After that, a game carries on even if the server goes offline, and the server's work per move is only to queue what the browsers report.

### Starting a game

Matchmaking stays in the database (`find_match`, below). Once paired, the two browsers exchange connection details through a private Realtime channel (about 10 messages) and open a direct, encrypted WebRTC data link, finding a path through home routers with free public STUN servers. The set-up channel is private to the two players, so each knows the link really leads to their opponent.

### During the game, over the direct link

- **Moves** carry the ply number, the move, the mover's clock and a hash of every move so far, so the browsers notice at once if their records ever differ. Each browser checks every move with `xiangqi-core`; an illegal move is refused and becomes a dispute.
- **Clocks** are kept by both browsers. The time charged for a move is what the opponent saw (from sending their move to receiving the reply) minus the network delay, measured from the heartbeats' round trip, within the lag allowance. The mover's own figure stands when the two agree within that allowance, which is almost always.
- **Heartbeats** every second or two cost nothing, because they don't touch the server, so each browser knows within seconds if the other has gone (D6).
- **Draw offers, resignations, takebacks and preset chat** go over the link too, and are reported like moves.

### Reports to the server, through a message queue

After each move, each browser reports to the server what it sent or received: ply, move, its clock readings and the hash. At the end it reports its own replay of the whole game and the result.

- Reports go into a **message queue** (Supabase Queues, the `pgmq` Postgres extension, included in the free plan), not straight into tables. A report is one quick insert, stamped with the time it arrived; that time stamp is the server's own, independent timeline of the game.
- Sending never holds up play. If it fails, the browser keeps the report in an outbox and sends it later, as the site already does for account data.
- A **queue worker** (a database function run by pg_cron every few seconds) reads reports in batches: it writes the game log and the fair-play data, notices games that have ended, and hands them to `finish`. A report it can't process yet stays in the queue and is retried; processed reports are archived, so nothing is lost.
- The queue absorbs bursts (many games ending at once, or a backlog after an outage), and the worker or `finish` can change without touching the browsers.

### Disputes

When the browsers can't agree (a move one calls illegal, a clock difference beyond the allowance, a time-out the other denies, or a lost link), both send their evidence (moves, clock readings, timings) to the `judge` function, which compares it with the reports in the log:

- The browser whose account matches the server's arrival time stamps wins the point; the other's is set aside. An illegal move loses the game.
- If the log has a gap (the server was offline), the judge decides from the two browsers' evidence alone where they agree, and otherwise ends the game with **no result and no rating change**.
- A player whose browser keeps ending up on the wrong side of disputes is flagged for fair-play review: an honest browser doesn't make up clock readings.

### If the server goes offline mid-game

Play continues over the direct link, and reports wait in each browser's outbox. The result is recorded and rated when the server is back. A dispute that arises meanwhile waits too; play pauses only if the two browsers can't agree whose move it is.

### At the end

Each browser replays the whole game itself and shows the result at once. The `finish` function replays it again from the log to rate both players. If a browser's replay differs from the log's, the log decides and that browser is flagged.

### When a direct link can't be made

Some mobile networks and strict firewalls block direct links; about 1 game in 10 is typical. The same relay is used for players who turn on "Don't share my network address" in Settings → Play, and always for players under 16, because a direct link shows each player's internet address to the other. Those games are relayed through a private Realtime Broadcast channel instead, with the same rules and reports; only the path differs, and they cost about 340 Realtime messages each. A TURN relay server would avoid this, but no free TURN service is certain to stay free, so it's left out.

### The rest

- **Matchmaking** is a seeks table and a `find_match` function that pairs two seekers in one transaction (`FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED`), called every few seconds while searching so the window can widen.
- **Abandoned games:** the queue worker ends games whose reports stopped and that neither player resumed, aborts games whose first move never came, and clears old seeks.
- **New tables:** `games` (players, time control, result), `game_log` (one row per move and browser, with the arrival time stamp), the `game_reports` queue, `seeks`, `online_ratings`, `game_analysis`, `reports`, `disputes`, `fairplay` (per-player scores and standing), `mod_log`, `blocks`. Written only by database and edge functions; row-level security as in v0.2.

### Free-plan budget

| Resource | Free plan | Use per game (100 plies) | Games it allows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Realtime messages | 2 million a month | about 15 with a direct link; about 340 when relayed | **about 60,000 a month** if 1 game in 10 is relayed (was 5,800 with every move relayed) |
| Realtime connections | 200 at once | only while finding a game and setting up the link, or for a relayed game | several hundred games at once |
| Database calls | not limited | about 200 small queue inserts (each browser reports each move) | Postgres handles this easily at hobby scale |
| Edge function calls | 500,000 a month | 1–2, plus 1 per dispute | far more |
| Database | 500 MB | about 10 KB of log plus analysis | about 40,000 games stored; a screened game's log can be shrunk to one row |
| Egress | 5 GB a month | about 50 KB | about 100,000 a month |

To stay inside it: no spectating in v0.3, no global "who's online" Presence (the count comes from a database query), and a monthly check of the usage page. On the free plan going over doesn't cost money; Supabase warns, then restricts.

## Open questions

- [x] Network addresses: settled 2026-10-12. Direct links show each player's internet (IP) address to the opponent's browser, enough for a rough location or to attack the connection. Games link directly by default; a setting "Don't share my network address" uses the relay instead, and players under 16 are always relayed.
- [ ] Names for the speeds in Chinese (闪电 / 超快棋 / 快棋): native-speaker check.
- [x] Rated draw offers: settled 2026-10-11, from 10 moves each.
- [x] Bot-game draw minimum: lowered from 20 to 10 moves each on 2026-10-11, so there's one rule everywhere.
- [x] Who can play online: settled 2026-10-11 (D9).
- [x] Bot rating calibration: checked on 2026-10-12 (online starting ratings and the D9 check both rely on bot ratings). The bots played each other with the site's engine and move choice, 400 games (`packages/bots/scripts/calibrate.mjs`, results and fitted ratings in `packages/bots/calibration/`). Every bot beats the one below it, so the ladder's order holds. The gaps were uneven: Old Chen to Meiling measured 470 points against a labelled 200. Search depths changed to even them out: Meiling 6 → 5, Aqiang 8 → 7, Teacher Lin 10 → 9. Xiaoyu to Dragon King now fit their labels; Teacher Lin is at the edge (fitted 1766, range 1603–1982), and its remaining gap above Aqiang comes from making no random blunders, not from search depth. The two beginner bots measure weaker than their labels (Little Soldier about 0, Grandpa Fu about 230), but those numbers depend on where the average is fixed, which bot games can't tell. The labels stay as they are. Bot-vs-bot games measure only the gaps and tend to stretch them, so once online play has data, real players' games against the bots will set the bots' ratings.
- [ ] Moderators besides the owner, later: who, and how they're trusted.
